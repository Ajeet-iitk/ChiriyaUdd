How it's played

A game older than the internet

Somewhere between a lullaby and a prank. Children in courtyards across South Asia have played a version of this for generations — with one finger, and no equipment at all.

The evening is warm. Someone opens a palm, face up, and everyone else rests an index finger on it. One caller — the ustad — begins.

“Chidiya.” The fingers rise, like a small flock startled off a wire. “Kursi.” Nothing moves. A chair cannot fly, and everyone knows it.

Then the caller gets quicker, and meaner: a sparrow, a table, an aeroplane, a penguin. The palm becomes a lie detector. The first person to lift for a chair — or to stay for a parrot — loses, and the circle exacts its affectionate revenge.

It is a game about attention, and about trusting your own hand. It is also, mostly, an excuse to laugh at your friends.

“The finger knows before the mind does.”

— every courtyard, ever
  1. 01

    Rest your finger

    Press and hold the palm with your mouse, thumb, or the space bar. This is your bird, perched.

  2. 02

    Listen for the call

    A name appears. Decide in one breath whether that thing can fly, and let the ring tell you how long you have.

  3. 03

    Lift, or stay

    If it flies, lift your finger before the ring closes. If it doesn't, keep it planted. Then place it back for the next name.

  4. 04

    Take your thwacks

    A wrong call costs a feather, a giggle, and a flurry of friendly hands. Three feathers gone and the baithak is over.

Two ways to be wrong

Lift for a chair, or stay for a parrot. The courtyard is not picky about which.

Wings are not a promise

Penguins, ostriches, emus and kiwis have feathers and no flight. They are the classic trap.

Machines count

Aeroplanes, rockets, drones and paper planes all fly. A submarine, however, has other ambitions.

When in doubt, watch the ring

The ring is your breath. Lift early; the game never punishes a fast, correct hand.
